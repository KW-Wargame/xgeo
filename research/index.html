ABSTRACT

xGEO simulates a gray-zone confrontation in low Earth orbit. Its scenario, CLOSE PASS, is built on a real event: in October 2026 the Russian satellite Cosmos 2612 shadowed ICEYE-X36, a commercial radar-imaging satellite, at a few kilometres. The player commands WATCHMAN-1, a notional US inspector, and has to make Cosmos 2612 break off without contact, debris or open conflict, while keeping track of it with real ground radars. The craft start from public SGP4 element sets and fly under the Earth's gravity with its J2 flattening, the Sun and the Moon. This edition sets out every physical model the engine uses, with its equation, its source, how sure we can be of it, and which numbers are design values chosen for play. The engine's code is checked against JPL Horizons, hapsira, scipy, astropy, filterpy, ITU-R recommendations and published worked examples: 681 comparisons across 64 tests, 680 within tolerance, and 4 further rows that report known simplifications. Section 11 lists what could not be verified.

1Method and labels

The rule is that every number is either sourced or labelled. Each source claim carries one of four labels:

  • V-TEXT The document was fetched and the passage read; the section, table or page is given.
  • V-RECORD The work exists (publisher, DOI or official listing) but the passage was not read, because of a paywall or because it is a book. The equation used is the standard textbook form.
  • UNVERIFIED Not confirmed. Treat it as unsourced.
  • DERIVED Computed from verified inputs, with the method stated so it can be rerun.

Values chosen for play carry DESIGN VALUE. A design value is never presented as measured. Where possible, it is chosen inside a range that a source does support. The most important design values in CLOSE PASS are the inspector's plan (where it parks and for how long), the distances at which it gives way or leaves, the three hours of lost contact after which it withdraws (only with a Blue craft within 25 km of it), the tension costs, and the scenario's two days, against the weeks the real event took.

The game runs in Godot 4.7 (GDScript, with 64-bit arithmetic wherever precision matters). Its physics is tested by an external suite (§10), which reads the engine's own outputs. Python never reimplements the game in order to grade it.

2Frames, time and starting states

2.1 Reference frame and time

CLOSE PASS is centred on the Earth. Its axes are those of GCRF, the Earth-centred frame aligned with the ICRF: the Earth's mean equator and equinox of J2000 to within the frame bias of a few milliarcseconds. The Earth is an oblate body, 6,378.137 × 6,356.752 km (WGS 84), with GM = 398,600.4418 km³ s⁻² and J2 = 1.08262668×10⁻³ (EGM2008, as tabulated by Vallado18). The 3-D view uses a floating origin at the camera's focus, so positions keep their precision at any distance. The clock is UTC, starting at 21:00 UTC on 26 October 2026 and running two days. Leap seconds, TT − UTC and UT1 − UTC are ignored; §2.4 gives what that costs.

2.2 Starting states from public element sets

ICEYE-X36 (NORAD 59103, 2024-043F) starts on its real orbit. Its CelesTrak GP element set (epoch 26 October 2026, 21:06 UTC)166 is propagated by SGP4 (Vallado et al. 2006167), whose output is in the TEME frame, and then rotated into GCRF. The result is a near-circular orbit with semi-major axis 6,924.4 km: 549 km up, a period of 95.57 minutes and a speed of 7.584 km/s. SGP4 is used only for this starting state. From then on every craft is integrated under the force model of §3, which is how a real analyst would turn a catalogue orbit into a precise one.

Cosmos 2612 (NORAD 68762, 2026-083H) starts 5.1 km behind ICEYE-X36 and 140 m above it, where public element sets put it at 07:38 UTC on 18 October 2026 (KeepTrack's account of the rendezvous168). It is placed on the path that does not drift away along-track, which is how an inspector holds station. Blue starts with Cosmos 2612's catalogued orbit, about 200 m off its true one (a design value). WATCHMAN-1, the notional US inspector, starts 20 km behind ICEYE-X36.

2.3 The Sun and the Moon

The Sun's position comes from JPL's approximate Keplerian elements for the Earth–Moon barycentre (Standish & Williams2, Table 1, valid 1800–2050), reversed to give the Sun as seen from the Earth: 0.9938 AU at the start. The barycentre lies about 4,700 km from the Earth's centre, which turns the Sun's direction by under 7 arcseconds. The Moon comes from the low-precision lunar series of Montenbruck & Gill20 (§3.3.2, eqs. 3.47–3.48), good to a few arcminutes, about 500 km at the Moon's distance. It is 367,065 km away at the start. GM values come from DE4403 (the Moon's 4,902.800118 km³ s⁻²). Planets are added only beyond the Earth's sphere of influence, where their pull starts to matter.

2.4 The turning Earth and its ground sites

The ground radars of §4.5 and Cosmos 2612's handler near Moscow sit on the turning Earth. A site's position is rotated from the Earth-fixed frame into GCRF by Greenwich mean sidereal time (GMST 1982, Vallado18 Alg. 15) and by IAU 1976 precession (Alg. 23), at the Earth's rate of 7.292115×10⁻⁵ rad/s. Nutation (under 20″), polar motion (under 0.5″) and UT1 − UTC (under 0.9 s) are omitted, so a site sits within about 1 km of where a full IERS reduction would put it. A radar measures what it sees from that point; a 1 km site error is about a thousandth of a typical range, and it is shared by every look from that site.

3Gravity and trajectory design

3.1 Force model and integration

The acceleration on a craft is the Earth's point mass, its J2 zonal term, and the Sun and the Moon as third bodies (direct minus indirect term; Montenbruck & Gill20 §3.3, Cowell's method):

a = −μ r/|r|³ + aJ2(r) + Σk μk [ (rk − r)/|rk − r|³ − rk/|rk|³ ]

Integration is DOP853, the Dormand–Prince 8(5,3) pair with error control (Hairer, Nørsett & Wanner 1993), every step held to a relative 10⁻¹², so steps lengthen on quiet arcs and shorten near a close pass, with no step rule to tune. Against scipy's DOP853 at 10⁻¹³ on the same forces, the engine stays within 2.8 mm over a day, and within 0.13 m over a 20-day J2 arc. The classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta step stays in the code for tests. There is no atmospheric drag (§11). The same engine, run on its own, centres the world on Jupiter with the four Galilean moons as its third bodies; the external suite of §10 checks that world against JPL Horizons.

For the Earth, two checks run in Godot on every change. ICEYE-X36's orbital node turns 1.0395° per day, against 1.0400° from J2's first-order secular rate (Vallado eqs. 9-41/42). And a craft placed on the no-drift path beside it stays within 779 m of it over a whole orbit, which shows that the starting states and the forces agree.

3.2 Two-body tools

Kepler propagation uses universal variables (Curtis17 Alg. 3.3–3.4), with Danby's starter near e = 1 and Vallado's starter for long hyperbolic coasts (Vallado18 Alg. 8). Lambert's problem is solved by Izzo's method (Izzo 201523), including multi-revolution transfers. Across 111 test transfers it agrees with hapsira's Izzo solver to 7.3e-15, and it reproduces Curtis Example 5.2. An earlier build used Curtis Alg. 5.2; a loss of precision in its Stumpff function as z → 4π² made every transfer over 180° fail, which was one reason for the change.

3.3 Refining under full gravity

Over a 3-hour coast in low orbit, J2 alone moves a craft by kilometres, so a two-body Lambert solution misses a target only a few kilometres away. Every planned burn is therefore refined by differential-correction shooting through the full-gravity coast (Vallado18; single shooting as set out in NASA/TP-20220014814 §5.2.1). Newton's method starts from a two-body Jacobian and updates it with Broyden's method24. A 3-hour move by WATCHMAN-1 from 20 km behind ICEYE-X36 to beside it is planned at 0.74 m/s and flies at 0.75 m/s.

3.4 The planner

The planner shows a porkchop plot: departure wait against time of flight, each cell a refined transfer coloured by the Δv to go and the Δv to match on arrival. Its grid steps are design values. Close to a target, the planner also offers station-keeping: holding a point a set distance ahead, behind, above or beside the target. A point 1 km out of ICEYE-X36's plane is held at 1.00 km.

3.5 Leaving Earth orbit

Where a scenario allows it, the planner plans between planets with patched conics (Curtis ch. 8; Vallado ch. 12). A heliocentric Lambert arc runs between the planets; the escape burn is fitted so its hyperbola leaves from the ship's actual position; and the plan is refined under full gravity. Spheres of influence follow Laplace, r = a(m/M)2/5 (Curtis §8.4). From ICEYE-X36's orbit, the cheapest departure for Mars in the 400 days after the start costs 4.03 km/s. WATCHMAN-1 carries 259 m/s, Cosmos 2612 about 90. Interplanetary flight is switched off in CLOSE PASS, and these figures show it would be out of reach anyway.

4Radio and radar: antennas, links and detection

4.1 Antenna gain and patterns

The peak gain of a dish of diameter D at wavelength λ is G₀ = η(πD/λ)², with η = 0.55 throughout. That is 1.24 dB conservative against the DSN 34 m antenna at X band (68.24 dBi at 8425 MHz, DSN 810-005 Module 104 Rev. O Table 839). The beamwidth is about 70λ/D degrees (NAWCWD TP 8347 §3-154; ITU-R SA.509-3 §1.333) V-TEXT.

Off-axis gain follows the ITU reference envelopes: SA.509-3 for large dishes (D/λ ≥ 100), and S.465-5 Note 4 for small ones34:

G(φ) = 52 − 10 log(D/λ) − 25 log φ dBi, 100λ/D ≤ φ < 48° G(φ) = 10 − 10 log(D/λ) dBi, 48° ≤ φ ≤ 180°

These are envelopes of sidelobe peaks, used in interference coordination, so they are conservative for a well-designed antenna. Note 2 of S.465 warns against using them below D/λ = 50, and every Red dish in the game is that small. They are still the most credible public pattern. Every emitter points. In CLOSE PASS, Cosmos 2612's command radio aims its 0.3 m dish at its handler near Moscow, and WATCHMAN-1 listens through a 0.5 m dish of its own. Warships and buoys in the engine's other world listen through four-face arrays that form beams in all directions (after the TDRS S-band multiple-access array, NASA TM-105422), with gain equal to the face's peak plus 10 log cos(scan).

Free-space loss is the exact 20 log(4πR/λ) of ITU-R P.525-4 eq. (3). The practical eq. (4) rounds its constant to 32.4 dB (exactly 32.448 dB), so the game differs from eq. (4) by 0.048 dB.

4.3 Detection of unknown bursts

Listeners don't know a burst's waveform, so they detect it with an energy (radiometer) detector (Urkowitz 196751). The integrated SNR grows as √(Bτ). The chance of detection is:

Pd = Q( (Q⁻¹(Pfa) − d) / √(1 + 2s) ), Pfa = 10⁻⁶

The false-alarm rate is a DESIGN VALUE. This expression matches the exact chi-square distributions to 0.01 for n ≥ 10⁵ samples, and even odds come at 6.8 dB. Spreading a signal over a wider band costs an eavesdropper's energy detector √(Bold/Bnew) in SNR at fixed power (the radiometer equation; Urkowitz 196751). ITU-R SM.105538 §1 notes that spread signals are noise-like and not readily detected.

4.4 Direction finding and identification

A two-element interferometer measures a bearing with

σθ = λ/(2πd) · σφ, σφ² = (1 + 2s) / (2Bτ s²)

This is the cross-correlation phase error for a noise-like signal. It follows from the Knapp & Carter (1976) Cramér–Rao bound in terms of coherence, γ² = s²/(1 + s)², in the narrowband limit; it is checked by a Monte Carlo of a two-element correlator to 3%. A 20 µrad floor is a DESIGN VALUE. Bearings from separated listeners are crossed by weighted least squares. Identification compares a recording's modulation and symbol rate against a threat library above a minimum SNR, a design-value gate. Specific-emitter identification is real: it recognises a device by imperfections in its analog circuitry (Danev et al.78).

4.5 Ground radar

Five real radar sites track the three craft. Each look returns range, azimuth and elevation, each with Gaussian noise, every 10 seconds while the craft is above the site's elevation mask and inside its reach. A radar at a site differs from one on a ship in two ways that matter here. It sees a craft only for a few minutes per pass, so custody between passes rests on the filter's prediction (§7). 5Electromagnetic attack and tension

The engine models jamming, spoofing, dazzling, high-power microwaves, cyber takeover and kinetic kills, and its other world uses them all. CLOSE PASS gives Blue none of them: WATCHMAN-1's only lever is its presence. The models are kept here because they are part of the engine, and because a scenario that adds a jammer would rely on them.

5.1 The jamming equation

Jamming a command uplink means being louder, at the victim's receiver, than its handler. Both paths are one-way, so the equation is Friis applied twice. This is the communications form of NAWCWD TP 8347 §4-9 V-TEXT, and Adamy's EW 10175 gives it in dB form:

J = Pj + Gj(aim error) + Gv(θj) − Lj S = Pu + Gu + Gv(0) − Ls J/S = J − S − 10 log(Bj/Bv) dB

Gv(θj) is the victim's gain toward the jammer, at angle θj off its line to the handler, so geometry matters: a jammer near the handler's line faces the victim's main beam. The aim term means a loose track costs beam-pointing gain. Before the signal is identified, a jammer must spread its power across the whole allocation (barrage); once identified, it can put it all in the channel (spot). How much J/S breaks a link has no universal number. NAWCWD §4-7 (p. 4-7.3) says plainly that effectiveness "is not a direct mathematical function of J/S" V-TEXT. The engine requires +6 dB, a DESIGN VALUE inside the 0–10 dB band typical for FEC-coded BPSK.

5.3 Escalation in the engine

Each effect carries an escalation cost, a design value: jamming 2 per hour, radar 1 per hour, spoofing 4, dazzling 8, cyber 10, high-power microwave 15, and a kinetic kill 45. Doctrine provides no escalation ladder (§8).

5.4 Tension in CLOSE PASS

CLOSE PASS has its own meter, because its only acts are approaches. Tension rises by 3 when Cosmos 2612 parks inside ICEYE-X36's 1 km keep-out, by 5 each time WATCHMAN-1 crowds it into giving way, and by 12 for every hour WATCHMAN-1 spends within 1 km of it. At 25, Cosmos 2612 turns cautious: it gives way once fewer before leaving, and it stops pushing in. At 50 it leaves at the next crowding. At 100 the confrontation has become open conflict and the scenario is lost. Every value is a DESIGN VALUE.

6Heat, infrared and visible signatures

6.1 Energy balance

Each craft has a skin and, if it carries a reactor, radiators. Skin temperature follows a single-node balance with heat capacity (Gilmore103):

C dT/dt = Pleak + α S f A/4 − ε σ T⁴ A, C = 897 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ × 0.25 × mdry

S = 1,361 W m⁻² at 1 AU (Kopp & Lean 2011109), scaled by the inverse square of the Sun's distance, and f is the sunlit share (§6.4). The heat capacity is aluminium's at 298 K, 24.21 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 897 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (NIST-JANAF, NIST Chemistry WebBook105); Incropera104 Table A.1 gives 903 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ at 300 K, 0.7% more. The 25% skin fraction is a DESIGN VALUE. WATCHMAN-1's skin has a time constant of about 1.5 hours (DERIVED: C = 897 × 0.25 × 600 kg = 134,550 J/K against 4εσT³A ≈ 25 W/K), so it cools noticeably during each pass through the Earth's shadow, which lasts up to about 36 minutes at 549 km. The Earth's own reflected sunlight (albedo 0.367) and its infrared glow (254 K as a black body) are not yet added to the heat balance (§11). Absorptance and emittance come from NASA RP-1121 (Henninger 198495).

The engine also models reactor radiators, after the 100 kWe Brayton plant of NASA Glenn's JIMO study (Mason 200396; Siamidis 200598), as two-sided Lambertian plates, I(θ) = εσT⁴A cos θ/π. No craft in CLOSE PASS carries a reactor.

6.2 Going dark

A reactor ship can stop radiating for a while by storing its heat in water it vents (2.5 MJ/kg) or ice it melts (333 kJ/kg, Leimkuehler 2011102). This is part of the engine's other world; CLOSE PASS has no use for it.

6.3 In-band infrared detection

Telescopes work in NEO Surveyor's NC2 band, 6–10 µm (Mainzer et al. 2023115 V-TEXT). The in-band share of each body's emission comes from the Widger & Woodall series, which matches astropy to 6×10⁻¹¹. The 0.5 m LWIR's 1σ noise-equivalent irradiance is 7×10⁻¹⁸ W m⁻². That lies inside the 0.4–1.1×10⁻¹⁷ range derived from NEO Surveyor's S/N-5 sensitivity. A look detects with

S/N = I / R² / (NEI × background), Pd = Φ(S/N − 5)

Against the planet's disc, the background raises the NEI ×50, a DESIGN VALUE inside the ×26–92 derived for a background-limited sensor. Just off the disc, stray light raises it ×5, also a design value, out to three times the planet's apparent radius but never more than 20° past its limb. From low orbit the Earth fills almost half the sky, and three times its apparent radius would leave no sky at all; 20° is about the Hubble Space Telescope's bright-Earth avoidance angle. False alarms are Poisson, with a mean of 0.1 per sensor per look (design value), and only lines from two places at once start a track.

6.4 Shadows and reflected light

The Sun is a limb-darkened disc, I(μ) = 1 − u(1 − μ) with u = 0.6 (Allen's106 §14.7), sampled by 397 rays in 12 rings. The sunlit share is the weighted fraction not blocked by the oblate Earth (6,378 × 6,357 km), the Moon, or, far out, a planet. This gives both umbra and penumbra (Montenbruck & Gill §3.4.2), within 0.019 of a dense numerical integration.

Sunlit hulls are also seen in visible light. The Sun's MV = 4.81 (Willmer 2018) is scaled to the irradiance at the craft, and each hull is a Lambert sphere with phase law Φ(α) = (sin α + (π − α) cos α)/π. It returns a share p·Φ(α)·r²/R² of the sunlight at its distance R, with p the geometric albedo and r² = A/4π for a hull of surface area A (Madhusudhan & Burrows 2012 eq. 3122). An earlier build used the cross-section πr² in place of r², which made every hull π times (1.24 mag) too bright and every visible range √π = 1.77 times too long. A 1 m GEODSS-class telescope reaches magnitude 22 (Stokes et al. 1996119; the free 1998 Lincoln Laboratory Journal account of the same tests gives magnitude 22 at S/N 4 in under 100 s). The game asks for S/N 5 at magnitude 22, a DESIGN VALUE. Visible telescopes look only where a track already exists, because reaching magnitude 22 takes about 100 s staring at one field.

7Tracking

Every contact is a track: an estimate with a covariance, not ground truth. Bearings and ground radar looks (range, azimuth and elevation, §4.5) update it in three dimensions through an unscented Kalman filter (Julier sigma points, κ = 0, circular mean for angles), which matches filterpy to 3×10⁻¹². Prediction uses central-difference state transition matrices and white-noise acceleration (Bar-Shalom et al. 2001). Two cases needed special handling.

  • Close in. With WATCHMAN-1 a few kilometres from Cosmos 2612, sigma points can straddle the sensor. Within a tenth of the range, a bearing therefore enters as a pseudo-linear measurement (Lindgren & Gong 1978; Aidala & Nardone 1982). Between craft in nearly the same orbit, range is unobservable from angles alone without a manoeuvre (Woffinden & Geller 2009). So without a recent radar range, the track keeps at least 0.5 × range of 1σ along the line of sight (design value).
  • Unseen burns. A bearing whose normalised innovation passes the χ²(1) 99.9% point (10.83) is taken as a burn nobody saw, and widens the track (Bar-Shalom ch. 11). The 50 m/s and 10 min used are design values.

CLOSE PASS scores custody: Blue's track of Cosmos 2612 within 1 km of the truth for at least 80% of the time (design values). Between radar passes the track coasts on the force model of §3, so custody depends on how well the filter predicts. In the unattended 24-hour run of Fig. 12, with no orders and only the radar sites looking, custody held 94% of the time. Tracks also size the burns they see: a jump in a track's motion is reported as a burn of about so many m/s, give or take its uncertainty.

A track nobody has detected for 24 h, or whose 1σ exceeds 100,000 km, is dropped (after Blackman & Popoli 1999). Both thresholds are design values. Contacts are drawn after MIL-STD-2525D, with a dashed frame for a last known position.

JP 3-85, JP 3-60 and AFDP 3-60 never use the words "spoofing" or "reversible": their terms are intrusion and deception, and temporary or nonlethal effects. (The USSF framework does speak of reversible and nonreversible actions, pp. 5 and 9.) Nor does it treat crowding another craft as an act at all. The tension meter of §5.4 is therefore entirely a design value. Its ordering (reversible and deniable acts cheap, debris-generating acts ruinous) follows doctrine's escalation-of-force guidance qualitatively.

9The scenario and its real-world basis

CLOSE PASS is built on a real uncooperative rendezvous and proximity operation. In October 2026 the Russian satellite Cosmos 2612 approached ICEYE-X36, a commercial X-band radar-imaging satellite, and shadowed it at a few kilometres. It was reported from LeoLabs tracking data by Breaking Defense and The War Zone on 19 October 2026158,159, and reconstructed from public element sets by KeepTrack168. As reported, the sequence was: coplanar alignment; closing from long range; station-keeping within 5 km, then 1–2 km; a closest pass reported at 500 m (another report gives 26 m); and moves ahead of and behind the target. The scenario keeps the real craft and their real orbits. WATCHMAN-1, the US inspector the player commands, is notional, and so is everything Cosmos 2612 does after the start.

  • Orbit. 549 km, a 95.57-minute period, 7.584 km/s (§2.2). Each orbit takes the craft through the Earth's shadow and over different radar sites.
  • Cosmos 2612's plan. In the order LeoLabs reported the real one: close in from behind to 2 km, reposition 2 km ahead, then loiter 0.8 km behind. Each move takes 3 hours and each slot is held for 6. Distances and times are DESIGN VALUES.
  • Coming closer. When no Blue craft is within 50 km of it, it takes its next slot at half the distance, but not under 0.5 km.
  • Giving way. With a Blue craft within 5 km of it, it gives way to a slot 8 km on the far side of ICEYE-X36. Crowded again after two give-ways (one in the tutorial), it leaves: 4 m/s prograde, and gone once it is beyond 200 km.
  • Its radio. An S-band (2.25 GHz) BPSK burst at 100 kbaud, 5 W into a 0.3 m dish aimed at its handler near Moscow (the Titov control centre is at Krasnoznamensk), two minutes long, every 3 to 6 hours and after each move. These are design values within ordinary practice.
  • Losing its link. Cut off from its handler for 3 hours within 200 km of its target, and with a Blue craft within 25 km of it, it leaves. Cut off but alone, it holds its slot and waits. This is grounded in practice, not in a published rule: a Progress approach to the ISS was aborted on loss of its telemetry link in 2010162, and ESA's ATV carried RETREAT and ESCAPE modes for the same reason. The threshold and the burn are DESIGN VALUES.
  • Score. Making it break off counts 40; keeping it outside 1 km of ICEYE-X36, 20; custody, 20; using no more than half of WATCHMAN-1's fuel, 10; staying below open conflict, 10. Design values.
  • Timescale. The real event took weeks. The scenario runs two days from 21:00 UTC on 26 October 2026 DESIGN VALUE.

Left alone, the plan runs to completion. Fig. 12 is a real 24-hour run of the engine with no orders given: Cosmos 2612 parks 2 km behind ICEYE-X36 at +3 h, passes within 490 m of it at +10.3 h on its way to its slot 2 km ahead (+11.5 h), and at +19.8 h parks 0.8 km behind, inside the keep-out. The keep-out objective fails, while WATCHMAN-1, given no orders, drifts from 20 km to 41 km behind.

10Verification

A Godot script runs every test case through the engine's own functions and writes the results out. A Python suite (pytest) then compares them with independent references, each comparison carrying its own tolerance. JPL data is cached, so the suite runs offline. The suite exercises the engine on its own world, at Jupiter, where JPL Horizons gives an exact reference for every body; the models are the same ones CLOSE PASS runs at the Earth. On 1 November 2026 it made 681 comparisons across 64 tests; 680 fall within tolerance, and 4 further rows report known simplifications. The one that fails is the check of who sees whom: it still assumes the glare round the planet's limb reaches three times its apparent radius, and predates the engine's cap of 20° past the limb (§6.3). Recomputed with the cap, it agrees on every pair the planet does not hide. The check, not the engine, is out of date, and it will be corrected.

11Limits, simplifications and open questions

Not public, so notional

  • Cosmos 2612's mass, engine, propellant, camera and radio. Only its orbit and its observed moves are public.
  • WATCHMAN-1 as a whole. GSSAP's mass is reported; its sizes, thrusters and sensors were never published.
  • Angle noise and elevation masks of the radar sites, and all of Space Fence's figures.
  • The site and mask of Cosmos 2612's handler.

Not verified

  • The closest approach of the real event: one report gives 500 m, another 26 m.
  • HawkEye 360 geolocation accuracy; the Albersheim validity limits; specific-emitter identification accuracy; look-through duty cycles.
  • The 2024 edition of JP 3-60 (the 2018 edition was read), and JP 5-0's definitions of MOE and MOP.
  • MIL-STD-2525D was read through a reproduction of its tables, not the original PDF.

Simplifications

  • No atmospheric drag. At 549 km it slows a small satellite by metres per second per year, depending on the Sun's activity; over two days it moves the three craft relative to each other by an amount that depends on their area-to-mass ratios, which are not public.
  • No reflected sunlight or infrared from the Earth in the heat balance, though the Earth's disc does raise the infrared background (§6.3).
  • A low-precision Moon (a few arcminutes) and an approximate Sun; no Earth gravity terms beyond J2.
  • No nutation, polar motion or UT1 − UTC: ground sites sit within about 1 km of their true places.
  • SGP4 is used only for the starting state; nothing is fitted back to element sets during play.
  • The umpire's view: some kill-chain indicators still read ground truth rather than Blue's assessed belief.

12.2 Running Core without the game

A command-line runner loads a scenario, steps time and writes what happens as JSON lines, one object per line:

godot --headless --path godot --script res://cli/run.gd -- \
      --hours=24 --step=600 --seed=4417 --out=run.jsonl

The first line describes the run (scenario, seed, start time, units). Each step line carries the time, escalation, Blue's ships (position, velocity and Δv left), Blue's tracks with their 1σ ellipses, and every log entry since the last step. The last line is the debrief. Tracks report Blue's belief, never the truth; --truth=true adds the true states for analysis. Units are kilometres, km/s and seconds, centred on the scenario's central body: the Earth in CLOSE PASS. Run with no scenario, Core loads its own sample world at Jupiter. The test suite runs it on every change.

12.3 Real, Projected and Notional

Every model and value carries one of three labels, which extend the source labels of §1:

  • Real: sourced and checked against an independent reference (§10). The orbits, gravity, the radio and radar equations, Planck's law, the filter.
  • Projected: near-future and grounded in programmes that exist today, such as nuclear-thermal drives at NASA's tested 900 s specific impulse.
  • Notional: invented for a scenario, the way defence and industry studies fly notional systems: Red's classes and behaviour, equipment sizes, escalation scores, the compressed timescale.

A Real model fed Notional inputs gives a Notional answer: the jamming equation is exact, but a Red jammer's power is a design value, and so is the range it yields. The project's fidelity table (docs/FIDELITY.md) gives each model's label, what it was checked against and what it leaves out.
